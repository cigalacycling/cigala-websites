H1: Critical Power Testing: How to Get Reliable Data
H2: Founder & Head Coach, Matteo Cigala
<img https://coaching.cigalacycling.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/download-2026-10-20T183643.559.png alt=''>
TXT: Critical Power (CP) testing is more than simply finding the highest average power you can produce.
P: A good CP test should give us a reliable estimate of your power-duration relationship, including CP and, depending on the protocol, W′ (W-prime).
P: The quality of the data depends heavily on how the test is performed.
<img https://coaching.cigalacycling.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/unnamed-5.jpg alt='Matteo Cigala doing indoor potentiations'>
H2: 1. Understand what we are actually testing
P: The CP model describes the relationship between the power you can sustain and the duration for which you can sustain it.
P: In its simplest form:
P: Work = CP × time + W′
P: Where:
LI: CP represents the sustainable aerobic power associated with the asymptote of the power-duration relationship.
LI: W′ represents a finite amount of work that can be performed above CP.
P: This is why we need maximal efforts of different durations.
P: A single 20-minute effort cannot properly describe your entire power-duration curve.
H2: 2. Choose appropriate test durations
P: For a traditional 3-point CP test, the efforts should be sufficiently separated in duration to provide useful information about the curve.
P: For example:
P: 3 min + 7 min + 12 min
P: or
P: 3 min + 12 min + 20 min
P: The exact protocol can vary, but the important principle is that the efforts should provide different physiological demands.
P: A very short effort is heavily influenced by anaerobic capacity and W′, while longer efforts increasingly reflect aerobic energy production and the sustainable portion of the power-duration curve.
P: The more appropriate the duration range, the more useful the resulting model.
H2: 3. The efforts need to be genuinely maximal
P: This is critical.
P: A CP model assumes that each trial represents a maximal effort for that duration.
P: If you ride a 5-minute effort at 95% of what you are capable of because you are afraid of blowing up, the resulting CP calculation can be distorted.
P: Equally, if you start far too hard and dramatically fade, the effort may be influenced by pacing error rather than your actual physiological capacity.
P: The objective is therefore:
P: Maximal sustainable power for the prescribed duration.
P: Not simply the highest power you can produce in the first 30 seconds.

H2: 4. Pacing is part of the test
P: Different durations require different pacing strategies.
P: For a short effort, you can tolerate a relatively high initial power because W′ contributes significantly to the effort.
P: For a longer effort, starting substantially above your sustainable level can deplete W′ too quickly and cause a large power decline.
P: A useful approach is to think about the effort as:
P: Aggressive → controlled → empty the tank
P: rather than:
P: Sprint → survive → fade
P: The objective is to minimise pacing error while still producing a maximal result.
H2: 5. Recovery between efforts matters
P: Recovery needs to be long enough to allow you to repeat a maximal effort, but the protocol should still be standardised.
P: The problem with insufficient recovery is obvious: the second and third efforts may become limited by residual fatigue and incomplete recovery of W′, rather than by the physiological capacity we are trying to measure.
P: However, excessive or inconsistent recovery can also affect the test.
P: Therefore, use the same recovery duration every time you repeat the protocol.
P: Consistency is extremely important when comparing tests.
H2: 6. Start the test in a controlled physiological state
P: Try to standardise:
LI: Sleep
LI: Nutrition
LI: Hydration
LI: Caffeine intake
LI: Time of day
LI: Training load beforehand
LI: Temperature
LI: Cooling
LI: Bike and power meter
P: You don’t necessarily need perfect laboratory conditions.
P: You need repeatable conditions.
P: If your first test is performed at 8am after a rest day in 15°C and your next test is performed at 6pm after a hard training block in 30°C, changes in the result become much harder to interpret.

H2: 7. Temperature and cooling are particularly important
P: Indoor testing can be significantly affected by thermoregulation.
P: During a maximal effort, metabolic heat production is very high. If cooling is inadequate, core temperature can rise and cardiovascular strain can increase.
P: A strong fan isn’t just about comfort.
P: It can influence your ability to sustain power.
P: Therefore, if you test indoors, use consistent and adequate airflow every time.
H2: 8. Don’t rely on FTP alone
P: One of the advantages of CP testing is that it gives us more information than a single threshold number.
P: Two riders can have a similar CP but very different:
LI: 1-minute power
LI: 3-minute power
LI: 5-minute power
LI: W′
LI: fatigue resistance
LI: power-duration curves
P: This is why I prefer looking at the entire power-duration relationship rather than simply asking:
P: “What is your FTP?”
P: CP gives us another way of understanding the interaction between aerobic power and finite work capacity above CP.
H2: 9. Look at the data, not just the result
P: After the test, I want to see the complete files.
P: Important variables include:
P: Power → duration → cadence → heart rate → pacing → W′ → CP
P: I also want to know how the efforts felt.
P: For example, two tests could produce the same CP but very different physiological responses.
P: The combination of objective data and subjective feedback gives us much more information than a single number.

H2: 10. Don’t test too frequently
P: Testing itself creates a significant training stimulus.
P: Repeated maximal efforts have a cost, particularly when using protocols involving several maximal efforts.
P: The purpose of testing is therefore not to constantly chase a new number.
P: The purpose is to measure adaptation.
P: If training is working, we should eventually see changes in the power-duration relationship.
P: That could mean:
LI: Higher CP
LI: Increased power at longer durations
LI: Increased W′
LI: Improved durability
LI: Reduced physiological cost at a given power
LI: Improved ability to repeat high-intensity efforts
P: The interesting question isn’t simply:
P: “Did my CP increase?”
P: It’s:
P: “How has my entire performance profile changed?”
P: The most important principle
P: A CP test is a measurement tool.
P: To make it useful, we need to control as many variables as possible and ensure that the efforts genuinely represent your maximal capacity for those durations.
P: Good testing = good data.
P: And good data allows us to make better training decisions.
